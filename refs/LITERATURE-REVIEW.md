# Literature review — OOD × GIS

40 papers, one paragraph each. Every entry follows the same four beats: what
problem the authors were reacting to, what they actually did, what the results
say, and where it breaks down. Limitations are the authors' own wherever they
stated them; where a paper states none, the limitation is prefixed
"Structurally," to mark it as a read of the setup rather than the authors' claim.

Companion file: `refs/README.md` indexes the same papers by *why each matters to
this project*. That split is deliberate — this file is about the papers on their
own terms. The synthesis at the end (§6) is where the two meet.

## 1. The OOD line — the method being ported, and the claim it disputes

The anchor paper and the five works it argues with. Read as a sequence, these
trace a claim being established (accuracy-on-the-line), stress-tested, and then
qualified three separate ways — by model-population diversity, by label noise,
and by disaggregation.

### OODSelect — Aggregation Hides Out-of-Distribution Generalization Failures from Spurious Correlations
`arXiv:2510.24884` · Salaudeen, Zhang, Alhamoud, Beery & Ghassemi / MIT, NeurIPS 2025

Accuracy-on-the-line has been read as reassurance that spurious correlations are rare or benign in practice: if ID accuracy predicts OOD accuracy, improving the former suffices. Salaudeen and colleagues argue the trend survives only because heterogeneous OOD examples get averaged together. OODSelect trains up to 4,200 diverse models per dataset — ResNets, DenseNets, ViTs, VGG, Inception, plus BERT, RoBERTa and GPT-2 for text — records a binary correctness matrix over OOD examples, then optimizes a relaxed selection vector with Adam to minimize the Pearson correlation between probit ID accuracy and subset OOD accuracy at a fixed subset size. On chest X-rays (No Finding), the full OOD set correlates at 0.86 while a 55,000-example subset covering 77% of it correlates at −0.60; a 25% Terra Incognita slice also inverts. PACS resists: its best 8% subset reaches only −0.33 against 0.81 for the full set. Selecting the most-misclassified examples instead yields near-zero correlations, separating spuriousness from difficulty. The authors flag the cost — thousands of models per dataset, a selection objective over roughly 146,000 elements — and that the subsets often defy semantic description, since many exploited features are imperceptible to humans.

### Accuracy on the Line — On the Strong Correlation Between Out-of-Distribution and In-Distribution Generalization
`arXiv:2107.04649` · Miller et al., ICML 2021

Domain adaptation bounds permit OOD accuracy to vary widely at fixed ID accuracy, and dataset reproductions of CIFAR-10 and ImageNet had shown near-perfect linear ID-OOD correlation — but only for reproductions and standard convolutional networks, leaving the scope unknown. Miller and coauthors ran more than 3,000 trained models through 100,000 test-set evaluations, spanning CNNs, vision transformers, self-supervised models, random features, kernel machines, random forests, kNN, and logistic regression, trained on CIFAR-10, ImageNet, FMoW-WILDS, iWildCam-WILDS, and a YCB-Objects pose task, with both axes probit-scaled. Linear fits are extremely tight: R² = 0.997 on CIFAR-10.2, 0.996 on ImageNet-V2, 0.991 on CINIC-10, 0.984 on FMoW worst-region accuracy, 0.990 on CIFAR-10-C fog. Varying hyperparameters, training duration, or training-set size slides models along the line without changing the fit. Camelyon17-WILDS breaks it — OOD accuracy spans chance to 95% at comparable ID accuracy — as does Gaussian-noise corruption. The authors state the trend as a conjecture, restrict it to ERM models trained in-distribution (zero-shot models follow a different line), and leave open whether raising ID accuracy is the best route to OOD gains.

### Teney et al. — ID and OOD Performance Are Sometimes Inversely Correlated on Real-world Datasets
`arXiv:2209.00613` · Teney et al., 2023

Wenzel et al. had reported observing no accuracy-robustness trade-off at all, and much of the literature treats ID performance as a legitimate model-selection criterion for OOD work. Teney and coauthors suspected the absence of inverse correlations was an artifact of how models were sampled. On WILDS-Camelyon17 they freeze a DenseNet backbone from ten pretraining seeds and retrain the last linear layer for one to ten epochs across ten retraining seeds, giving 1,000 ERM models, then repeat the retraining with a gradient-diversity-inducing objective that yields 24 models per run. Five further datasets — WildTime-arXiv, Waterbirds, CivilComments, WildTime-MIMIC-Readmission, WildTime-Yearbook — are trained with ERM, class balancing, mixup, selective mixup, and label-shift adjustment over ten seeds, recording every epoch. Within each pretraining seed, varying epochs produces a clearly decreasing ID/OOD trend that the diversity-trained models extend and sharpen; inverse patterns appear on four of the five extra datasets. Plotting only fixed-epoch ERM models across seeds reproduces the vertical scatter Wenzel et al. saw, which hides the effect entirely. The authors concede they examined one counterexample in depth, cannot estimate how common inversion is, and applied diversification only to linear heads rather than full networks.

### Accuracy on the Wrong Line — On the pitfalls of noisy data for out-of-distribution generalisation
`arXiv:2406.19049` · Sanyal et al. / MPI-IS, UCSD & Michigan, 2024

Prior counterexamples to accuracy-on-the-line were empirical and left the mechanism unexplained, so it remained unclear what actually has to be true for the correlation to invert. Sanyal and coauthors nominate two ingredients: interpolation of label noise and a large nuisance-feature space, with spurious shortcut features effectively manufacturing the latter. Empirically, Colored MNIST pairs a color-label shortcut with label-noise rate η and a three-layer MLP trained to zero training error; FMoW-CS, built on WILDS satellite imagery with a region-label spurious correlation and label noise 0.5, is used to fine-tune over 400 ImageNet-pretrained models (ResNet-18/34/50/101, DenseNet121) across learning rates, weight decays, and convolution widths. Theoretically, a lower bound on OOD-minus-ID error in a linear model grows with the model's nuisance sensitivity, which Proposition 3 shows rises with interpolated label noise. Correlation is positive at low η and negative at η = 0.2 and 0.25; on FMoW-CS noisy interpolators invert while noiseless interpolators and non-interpolating runs (early stopping, stronger regularization, narrower widths) stay positive. Scaling dataset size does not help. The authors note Proposition 3 covers only min-ℓ2 interpolators and leave other noise models and inductive biases open.

### Taori et al. — Measuring Robustness to Natural Distribution Shifts in Image Classification
`arXiv:2007.00644` · Taori et al., NeurIPS 2020

Robustness progress was being measured almost entirely on synthetic perturbations, and papers routinely claimed a robustness gain by showing higher accuracy on a shifted test set without separating that from higher standard accuracy. The study assembles 204 pretrained ImageNet models evaluated in 213 conditions — roughly 10⁹ predictions, a hundred times prior testbeds — across seven natural shifts grouped as consistency (ImageNet-Vid-Robust, YTBB-Robust pm-k), dataset shifts (ImageNetV2, ObjectNet, and the two anchor sets), and adversarially filtered (ImageNet-A), against 38 corruptions and PGD attacks. Two metrics are introduced: effective robustness, accuracy above the baseline linear fit, and relative robustness, the change versus the un-intervened model. Baseline fits are tight (r² = 1.00 ImageNetV2, 0.95 ObjectNet, 0.83 YTBB-Robust). Synthetic robustness barely predicts natural robustness: r = 0.24 for averaged corruptions, −0.05 for ℓp attacks. Only extra training data helps, marginally — ρ = 2.1% for ResNet152 on 11,000 classes, 1.5% for Instagram-1B ResNeXt, 1.1% for EfficientNet-L2, and −0.23% for a ResNet101 on the same JFT-300M. The authors caveat that extra data may contain renditions absent from ImageNet, and confine claims to ImageNet classification.

### OOD Evaluation Survey — A Survey on Evaluation of Out-of-Distribution Generalization
`arXiv:2403.01874` · Yu et al., 2024

Algorithmic work on OOD generalization — domain generalization, DRO, invariant learning, stable learning — had outpaced any systematic account of how to evaluate it, and the authors argue evaluation should answer not only whether a model generalizes but where: which shift types it handles and which input regions are unsafe. The review organizes the literature by test-data availability into three paradigms. OOD performance testing (labeled test data) covers synthetic dataset construction from causal graphs separating invariant from spurious covariates, real benchmarks, and shift-attribution tools including Shapley-value decomposition, DISDE's split into X-shifts and Y|X-shifts, WHYSHIFT, NICO++, SubpopBench, and OOD-Bench's correlation-versus-diversity shift axes. OOD performance prediction (unlabeled test data) spans confidence-based, distribution-discrepancy-based, and agreement-based estimators. Intrinsic property characterization (no test data) covers distributional robustness, stability, invariance, and flatness. The authors flag that prediction methods mostly presume covariate shift and are benchmarked only on ERM-trained architectures, and that absolute OOD comparisons can reward ID gains while the ID-OOD gap is no better, since heavy weight decay shrinks it by lowering ID accuracy. Structurally, the survey runs no unified experiments, so its comparative judgments are inherited from the cited work rather than tested.

### Group DRO — Distributionally Robust Neural Networks for Group Shifts
`arXiv:1911.08731` · Sagawa et al. / Stanford, ICLR 2020

Prior DRO work assumed convex models of limited capacity, which leaves it toothless for overparameterized networks: once a network drives both average and worst-group training loss to zero, optimizing the worst-case training objective changes nothing, and the real failure is a worst-group generalization gap. Sagawa and coauthors minimize worst-case expected loss over pre-specified groups, with group labels available during training only, using an online algorithm that interleaves SGD on parameters with exponentiated gradient ascent on group weights from per-example losses — O(1/√T) convergence when convex, under 5% runtime overhead. The key ingredient is strong regularization (ℓ2 penalties up to λ = 1.0, or early stopping), plus a C/√n_g group adjustment. On Waterbirds, CelebA, and MultiNLI with ResNet-50 and BERT, converged ERM hits 100% worst-group training accuracy but 60.0%, 41.1%, and 65.7% at test; naive DRO gives 76.9%, 41.1%, 66.4%. Under strong ℓ2, DRO lifts Waterbirds from 21.3% to 84.6% and CelebA from 37.8% to 86.7%; early stopping gives 86.0%, 88.3%, and 77.7%; adjustments reach 90.5% on Waterbirds. The authors note groups must be specified in advance, and call shifts beyond pre-specified groups important future work.

## 2. Geospatial OOD — what the GIS field already knows about distribution shift

A young and fast-moving literature: six of these eight appeared in 2026. The
common finding is uncomfortable for the field — domain-specific pretraining
buys little robustness, and the evaluations that would reveal this are not
standard practice.

### EarthShift — a benchmark for measuring robustness to real-world distribution shifts in Earth observation
`arXiv:2605.29330` · Doerksen & Kerner / Arizona State University, 2026

Satellite ML benchmarks report in-distribution accuracy, and earlier robustness work either used synthetic corruptions or probed one shift type on a handful of models, so the field had no picture of how EO models fail in deployment. Doerksen and Kerner assemble 11 tasks across five natural shifts: spatial scale (RESISC45 and UCMerced, both directions), temporal (Fields of the World, season-to-season and Germany 2018 to 2019), geographic (FTW Germany to Cambodia and to Denmark), sensor (BigEarthNetv2 and Sen1Floods11, Sentinel-2 to Sentinel-1; m-EuroSAT RGB to red-edge), and data source (DeepGlobe to DFC2022). Thirteen models — eight GFMs including CROMA, Clay, Prithvi V2, Galileo and TerraMind, three ImageNet/CLIP models, two random-init baselines — are scored by effective robustness, OOD performance minus a linear fit on ID performance, across 10,000+ runs at five seeds. Models lose 15–20% on average out-of-distribution. Domain pretraining buys nothing: ImageNet ResNet50 ranks most robust in both full fine-tuning and frozen settings, random-init ResNet50 second when frozen. Sensor shift hurts most, temporal shift barely registers, scale shift costs 43% of ID performance. Backbone size and fine-tuning strategy predict nothing. The authors note their shift coverage is not exhaustive, limiting general claims.

### GeoSpOT — OT on the Map: Quantifying Domain Shifts in Geographic Space
`arXiv:2604.16220` · Zhang, Betti, Klemmer, Rolf & Alvarez-Melis / Harvard & CU Boulder, 2026

Practitioners train where labels are plentiful and deploy where they are not, with no principled way to tell in advance whether the transfer will hold; geodesic distance ignores content, and feature-space dataset distances ignore geography. Zhang, Betti and colleagues define GeoSpOT, an optimal transport distance over a joint feature–location space whose ground cost mixes embedding distance with location distance, computed as an entropic Sinkhorn divergence. Feature embeddings come from ResNet50 and BERT, location embeddings from pretrained SatCLIP and GeoCLIP; raw geodesic distance is the baseline. Four settings are used: Geo-YFCC-Image, Geo-YFCC-Text, FMoW-Wilds (152,196 samples, 25 countries) and GeoDE (61,378 images, 19 domains). Every distance correlates negatively with the target-domain accuracy drop. ResNet50 features lead on Geo-YFCC-Image (|ρ| 0.71) and GeoDE (0.34); GeoCLIP alone leads on Geo-YFCC-Text (0.54) and FMoW-Wilds (0.65) from longitude and latitude alone. Combining both reaches 0.74, 0.58 and 0.38, while geodesic trails everywhere (0.42, 0.42, 0.24, 0.17). Greedy source selection under a 2,000-sample budget beats random selection for every target, favoring complementary domains — France and Portugal for Brazil — over nearest ones. The authors restrict their claims to image and text classification and call the selection experiments a feasibility demonstration.

### GeoFM Calibration — Beyond Accuracy: Assessing Calibration of Geospatial Foundation Models and Their Sensitivity to Distribution Shifts
`arXiv:2608.16614` · Lehmann et al. / Technical University of Munich, 2026

GeoFM leaderboards rank encoders by averaged clean accuracy, which says nothing about whether a confident prediction can be acted on. Lehmann and colleagues audited the same 152-paper GFM corpus and found not one reports a machine-learning calibration metric — every "calibration" mention is radiometric — nor do GEO-Bench, PANGAEA, REOBench or EarthShift. They probe 16 frozen encoders (Clay V1.5, Prithvi V2, DOFA, OLMo-Earth, TerraMind, Panopticon and DINOv3-Sat against ImageNet ResNets, Swin, ViTs and a random-feature baseline) with linear heads on four classification and five segmentation datasets under graded synthetic cloud, Poisson–Gaussian sensor noise and motion blur. Clean EO- and ImageNet-pretrained encoders are indistinguishable (0.847 vs 0.839 accuracy, 0.034 vs 0.038 ECE). Under cloud, mean accuracy falls from 0.84 to 0.34 at severity 3 and 0.15 at severity 5, while the clean ECE ranking survives at Kendall τ = 0.07 against 0.39 for accuracy. GeoFMs drift further into overconfidence at every grade (cloud-3 group ECE 0.400 vs 0.241). Temperature scaling does nothing under shift; a Gaussian-process probe halves severe-cloud ECE but triples clean ECE. The authors flag that synthetic corruptions do not replace real shift, backbones are never fine-tuned, and segmentation uses one decoder.

### GFM SOTA Audit — No One Knows the State of the Art in Geospatial Foundation Models
`arXiv:2605.12678` · Corley et al. / Taylor Geospatial & Arizona State University, 2026

GFMs are pitched as reusable backbones for disaster response, crop mapping and forest monitoring, yet the same encoder on the same benchmark appears with different numbers in different papers under nominally identical protocols, so no ranking can be read off the literature. Corley and co-authors audit 152 GFM papers from 2019–2025, seeded from prior surveys and expanded via OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar citation graphs plus a keyword sweep, extracting structured metadata from LaTeX source (140 papers) and Docling-converted PDFs (12) with LLM assistance, then a second LLM consistency pass and manual review. Thirty-nine percent release no model weights, another 19% code only. The corpus spans 401 distinct benchmarks; EuroSAT, RESISC45 and AID together cover 10.6% of evaluations, Gini 0.51, and 50 of 143 papers overlap zero of the top ten. Of 301 repeated (model, benchmark, metric, protocol, regime) tuples, 76 disagree by at least 5 points, 46 by 10, 20 by 20; Scale-MAE on RESISC45 under linear probing is reported at 33.0% and 89.6%, a 56.6-point gap. Across 126 papers specifying pretraining data, 94 use a configuration no one else repeats. The authors caution that extraction was LLM-automated and human error remains possible.

### GeoBS — Information-Theoretic Quantification of Geographic Bias in AI Models
`arXiv:2509.23482` · Wang et al. / Maine, Georgia, UT Austin & Microsoft Research, 2025

Existing geo-bias measures are either model-specific, like mean absolute deviation of LLM ratings, or spatially implicit, like averaging fairness scores over partitions — neither supports fair cross-model comparison or says which spatial factor produced the bias. Wang and colleagues recast any geo-bias metric as three choices (marked or unmarked point pattern, reference pattern, difference measure), reinterpret Unmarked and Marked Spatial Self-Information within it, then add three KL-divergence Spatial Relative-Entropy scores targeting multi-scalability (Scale-Grid), distance decay (Distance-Lag) and anisotropy (Direction-Sector). They evaluate 3 tasks, 8 datasets (iNat2017/2018, fMoW, MOSAIKS, EuroSAT, fMoW-sentinel, WorldStrat-LCCS/IPCC) and 8 models, including location encoders RBF, Space2Vec, NeRF and Sphere2Vec alongside GPT-4o, CROMA and SatMAE. Geo-bias does not track accuracy. For location-encoder models it is dataset-driven rather than model-driven: all models score low on iNat2018, high on Forest Cover. Foundation models score lower overall, but architecture dominates — CROMA fine-tuned outperforms SatMAE on three of four remote-sensing datasets while carrying far stronger bias of all three types (on fMoW-sentinel, Scale-Grid SRE 61.47 versus 2.16). The authors limit themselves to first-order relative-entropy scores, deferring network and space-time factors.

### Geographic Bias and Diversity in AI Evaluation
`arXiv:2606.05187` · Liu, Janowicz et al. / University of Vienna, 2026

Bias research has concentrated on gender and race, leaving the geographic dimension thinly examined and, more pointedly, leaving unanswered what an unbiased-in-space model would even look like. Liu and co-authors survey work spanning the pre-generative and generative eras, sorting geographic bias into representation bias in training data, aggregation bias in the form of MAUP scale and zoning effects, and discrimination on geographically loaded protected attributes; they then review geoparsing disparities, LLM factual recall, and the operationalization of geographic diversity through Hill numbers (order 0 richness, order 1 exponential Shannon entropy, order 2 inverse Simpson) and the similarity-sensitive Leinster–Cobbold number. Open Images draws 32% of samples from the United States and 60% from six North American and European countries, against 1% from China and 2% from India; ImageNet's 2011 fall release is 45% US, 1% China, 2.1% India. WorldBench found factual-recall error rates across 20 LLMs 1.5 times higher for Sub-Saharan African than North American countries. Generative models default to prototypes — Japan for GPT-3.5 Turbo, Canada for ChatGPT-4o, St. Stephen's Cathedral for Vienna — and diversity rises with sampling temperature across 297 categories and 10 models, while newer models are not more diverse. Structurally, this is a selective review rather than new measurement, much of the diversity evidence is the authors' own prior work, and the chapter concedes the causal link from imbalanced benchmarks to downstream performance disparities has still not been systematically evaluated.

### Mobility Domain Shift — Quantifying geographic domain shift to decouple the geospatial transferability of human mobility flow generation models
`arXiv:2608.21567` · Zhou, Gao et al. / UW–Madison & Zhejiang University, 2026

Transferability studies for mobility generation assume the target region differs from the training region without quantifying how much, so reported cross-region scores confound model design with how far apart the regions happen to be. Zhou and colleagues run leave-one-state-out transfer for four origin-destination flow models — DeepGravity, random forest, GBRT and GMEL — trained on one state and evaluated on counties of the remaining 47, using the CommutingODGen census-tract-level benchmark covering 2,265 counties in 48 states. They propose two shift metrics: mutual-information shift for feature distributions, and a signed Moran's I spatial shift for spatial-autocorrelation structure. Transferability is measured by common part of commuting and RMSE, with associations fit by linear mixed-effects regression. DeepGravity leads (CPC 0.603 ± 0.101, RMSE 82.5). CPC exceeds 0.70 in the Midwest but sits at 0.33–0.48 along the coasts, and mean CPC across source states spans only about 0.58 to 0.65 — a 0.07 range exceeding published gaps between competing architectures. Transfer is asymmetric, with CPC asymmetry from 0 to 0.2. MI shift is negatively associated with CPC and positively with log RMSE, Moran shift the reverse, all p < 0.001 and sign-consistent across all four models; training-set size is not significant. The authors note reliance on one dataset in one country, the need to validate on other generation models, and that global Moran's I misses finer structure such as polycentric versus monocentric patterns.

### GeoFM Agriculture — Benchmarking Geospatial Foundation Models for Agriculture Applications
`arXiv:2606.29664` · Shang, Das & Eldawy / UC Riverside, 2026

Crop-mapping benchmarks are confined to Europe (PASTIS, BreizhCrop, Sen4AgriNet, EuroCropsML) or destroy the spatial structure needed to test transfer by randomly subsampling chips (GEO-Bench); PANGAEA tests regional transfer only on a single urban land-cover dataset, and PhilEO-Bench is not agricultural — so whether GeoFMs transfer across U.S. agricultural geographies was untested. Shang and co-authors fine-tune Prithvi, SpectralGPT and SatMAE for multi-temporal segmentation over 13 Cropland Data Layer classes and 2023-to-2024 change detection, stacking six Sentinel-2 bands at three season points into 18-channel chips. Each of Iowa, North Carolina, California and Minnesota is cut into three non-overlapping 800–1,300 km² regions assigned to train, validate and test. SpectralGPT's crop mIoU drops from 25.95 in North Carolina to 7.45 in California; Prithvi averages 4.55 across states and reaches 0.02 in California. Winter wheat and sorghum are near zero everywhere, and aggregate mIoU is inflated by forest, water and developed classes. Change-detection F1 peaks in Minnesota (86.05 SpectralGPT, 83.26 Prithvi) and bottoms out in California (18.73 and 27.00), with region ordering consistent across architectures. The authors state each model was run once so differences are untested for significance, SatMAE segmentation covers only two states with a gap they are still auditing, and decoder heads and input adaptation differ across models.

## 3. Slice discovery — finding and naming the sets

Eleven papers spanning 2018 to 2026. The arc is unusually clean: the field solved
*finding* coherent failure sets early and has spent the years since fighting over
how to *describe* them. Every method here is some arrangement of embed → cluster →
name, and in almost every paper the limitations section is about the naming step.

### Slice Finder — Automated Data Slicing for Model Validation: A Big Data - AI Integration Approach
`arXiv:1807.06068` · Chung et al. / Google Research, MIT & KAIST, ICDE 2019

Model validation tools of the time assumed a domain expert could name the sub-populations worth checking, or at least the feature to slice by; the generic alternative, clustering, returns groups with high feature variance and cardinality that a human cannot summarize or act on. Slice Finder defines a slice as a conjunction of feature-value literals and scores it by comparing its loss against its complement with Welch's t-test plus an effect size normalizing that difference by pooled variance, returning top-k slices ordered by fewer literals, larger size, larger effect. Two searches are offered: a decision tree, which partitions and so cannot return overlapping slices, and a breadth-first lattice search, which can; alpha-investing controls marginal false discovery across the many tests. On UCI Census Income and Credit Card Fraud with injected label-flipped slices, lattice search beat the tree, which beat clustering, whose average effect size hovered near zero; a 1/128 sample retained 0.88 relative accuracy, and top Census slices were single-literal (Married-civ-spouse, size 14,065, effect 0.58). The authors leave numeric discretization, slice merging and summarization, and a user study on whether these slices actually help debugging to future work.

### GEORGE — No Subclass Left Behind: Fine-Grained Robustness in Coarse-Grained Classification Problems
`arXiv:2011.12945` · Sohoni et al. / Stanford, NeurIPS 2020

Group DRO raises worst-case performance across subpopulations only when group identities are labeled, and hidden stratification is precisely the case where the damaging subclasses inside a coarse class were never annotated; prior unsupervised work either discovered categories without optimizing worst-case risk or assumed the labels it needed. GEORGE trains a standard ERM model, extracts penultimate-layer features, reduces them with UMAP or the component orthogonal to the decision boundary, and clusters within each superclass using a Gaussian mixture, choosing k by Silhouette score and overclustering then filtering small sub-clusters; those assignments become groups for a second GDRO run whose checkpoint is picked by worst-cluster validation accuracy. Waterbirds robust accuracy rises from 63.3% to 76.2% and CelebA from 40.3% to 53.7%, cutting average worst-case subclass error by 22% against the oracle's 51%; clusters recover Waterbirds' rare subclasses at recall 0.91 and 0.93 but precision 0.19 and 0.33, and surface an unlabeled histopathology subclass in ISIC. The authors note the Silhouette criterion has several flaws, wrongly rejecting the BiT embeddings that work best on CelebA, and that on ISIC accurate clusters bought no gain because even the oracle failed there.

### Spotlight — The Spotlight: A General Method for Discovering Systematic Errors in Deep Learning Models
`arXiv:2107.00758` · d'Eon et al. / UBC, Vector Institute, Dalhousie & Alberta, AAAI 2022

Auditing a model for group disparities normally requires the practitioner to name and label the groups in advance, so failure modes nobody anticipated are never surfaced. GEORGE, the closest prior method, clusters within each class separately, cannot find problems spanning classes, and its superlinear clustering does not scale. The spotlight instead reweights the dataset in a model's final hidden layer: a truncated quadratic kernel centered at a learnable point, whose center and precision Adam optimizes to maximize weighted mean loss under a soft barrier enforcing minimum total weight, in linear time. Further spotlights come from discounting already-covered losses. On FairFace, ImageNet, Amazon polarity, MovieLens 100k, SQuAD, and chest x-rays, spotlights isolated profile views, children, and dark-skinned faces in poor lighting; ImageNet superclasses; Spanish-language Amazon reviews the English tokenizer mishandles; and SQuAD's two highest-loss topics. The first spotlight carried higher average loss than any of 50 or 20 Gaussian-mixture clusters on three of four datasets, each optimized in under a minute. The authors report that x-ray and SQuAD results were ambiguous, that a model relying on spurious backgrounds would likely defeat the method, and that finding nothing proves nothing.

### Domino — Discovering Systematic Errors with Cross-Modal Embeddings
`arXiv:2203.14960` · Eyuboglu, Varma & Saab et al. / Stanford, ICLR 2022

Slice discovery methods existed, but nothing established whether they worked: evaluations were qualitative, synthetic, or confined to a few tasks, and the qualitative ones showed discovered slices were often incoherent, united by no concept a domain expert would recognize. The authors programmatically generate 1,235 slice discovery settings from base datasets carrying class hierarchies or metadata (CelebA, ImageNet, MIMIC-CXR, an EEG seizure corpus), spanning rare, correlation, and noisy-label slices, each with a trained or synthetic model. Domino embeds inputs with cross-modal encoders (CLIP, ConVIRT, MIMIC-CLIP, EEG-CLIP), fits an error-aware Gaussian mixture jointly over embeddings, labels, and predictions by EM, then names each slice by matching a class-subtracted prototype against a text corpus. It recovers 36% of ground-truth slices, 12 points above prior methods, and ranks the exact slice name first in roughly 35% of settings; CLIP reaches 0.570 mean precision-at-10, 23 points over random activations, and ConVIRT 0.765 on MIMIC-CXR against 0.695 for BiT. A naive confusion-matrix baseline still wins on correlation slices. The authors caution that Domino will miss some failure modes, giving false security, and that its web-trained embeddings may encode biases governing which groups get flagged at all.

### InfEmbed — Error Discovery by Clustering Influence Embeddings
`arXiv:2312.04712` · Wang & Adebayo et al. / Meta and Prescient Design/Genentech, NeurIPS 2023

Prior slice discovery methods surface low-accuracy groups but never define what makes a slice coherent; "wrong for the same reason" was asserted through post-hoc captions, or approximated by a label-homogeneity hyperparameter that has to be tuned. InfEmbed formalizes it with influence functions. The influence explanation of a test point is the full vector of Koh–Liang influence scores of every training example on it, computed via an Arnoldi low-rank approximation of the inverse loss Hessian; a slice is coherent exactly when its members' influence explanations have low pairwise Euclidean distance, so slice discovery becomes the partition minimizing total within-slice distance between those vectors. Because that vector's dimension equals the training-set size, they substitute influence embeddings μ(z)=λ^(-1/2)Mᵀ∇L(z;θ), built so influence is their dot product, and prove by Cauchy–Schwarz that embedding distance upper-bounds explanation distance; InfEmbed is K-Means on these. On dcbench (1,235 models) precision@10 beats Domino everywhere except noisy-label EEG (natural images 0.65/0.55/0.67 vs 0.4/0.45/0.6), and coherence scores — the within-cluster K-means objective — improve in 1,219 of 1,235 settings, sometimes by nearly 50%. SpotCheck discovery rate is 0.91 vs PlaneSpot's 0.85, though false discovery rises to 0.15 from 0.07. The authors note the validation set must already contain the sought error, and users must still infer which slice-opponent feature causes it.

### LADDER — Language-Driven Slice Discovery and Error Rectification in Vision Classifiers
`arXiv:2408.07832` · Ghosh et al. / Boston University, 2024

Attribute-bank slice discovery converts images into precomputed tags and tests configurations of them, so it is capped by the bank, lacks the commonsense and domain reasoning specialized fields like radiology demand, and finds bias only in image attributes, never bias introduced during preprocessing or data preparation; Facts and DrML additionally require particular training setups. LADDER projects a frozen classifier's representation into a vision-language space (CLIP, CXR-CLIP, Mammo-CLIP), takes the per-class mean embedding difference between correctly and incorrectly classified samples, retrieves the top 200 (natural) or 100 (medical) caption or radiology-report sentences most similar to it, and prompts GPT-4o for bias hypotheses plus test sentences; images scoring below threshold on a hypothesis form its slice. Those same scores become pseudo-labels for a DFR-style linear-head fine-tune, ensembled across hypotheses. Across Waterbirds, CelebA, MetaShift, NIH CXR, RSNA- and VinDr-Mammo, Precision@10 improves roughly 50% over Domino and Facts on medical images; pneumothorax accuracy is ~98% with chest tubes versus 31% without, and worst-group accuracy reaches 91.4 on Waterbirds, 76.4 on RSNA (+7.3 over DFR) and 82.5 on VinDr (+21.1). The authors cite caption dependence, inherited CLIP/LLM bias, and subjectivity displaced into expert validation.

### SWD — Detecting Systematic Weaknesses in Vision Models along Predefined Human-Understandable Dimensions
`arXiv:2502.12360` · Gannamaneni et al. / Fraunhofer IAIS & Lamarr Institute, 2025

Existing SDMs work bottom-up: cluster in CLIP or model embedding space, then caption the cluster. The resulting slices are arbitrary and their descriptions often neither meaningful nor actionable, and nothing ties them to the safety-relevant dimensions a domain expert writes into an Operational Design Domain; structured-data slice finders cannot be applied to images at all because images carry no semantic metadata. SWD inverts this. ODD attribute dimensions are turned into CLIP zero-shot classification tasks, producing a structured metadata table over images or object crops, and SliceLine searches attribute-value conjunctions scored by error rate, size and complexity. Because CLIP labels are noisy, a Bayesian correction estimates the labeling function's precision and recall (60 human-annotated samples per attribute) and corrects observed error as Ê=(Ê_O+α−1)/(α+β−2); SWD-1 uses raw errors, SWD-2 corrected, SWD-3 merges both. On CelebA, all top-7 SWD-3 slices involve "wearing hat," the top-6 Oracle slices fall in SWD-3's top-7, error estimation improves from 0.30 off to 0.07, and recall over the top-60 reaches 100% at reduced precision; SWD-3 also discards the spurious "pale-skin" slice SWD-1 ranks first. Weaknesses in Faster R-CNN/BDD100k, SeTR/Cityscapes and Panoptic-FCN/RailSem19 concern occlusion, skin and clothing color, and age. The authors require a minimum metadata quality, note ODD incompleteness bounds what can be found, and limit scope to false negatives.

### DiffSub — Differential Subgroup Discovery: Characterizing Where Two Populations Differ, and Why
`arXiv:2604.27741` · Xu & Vreeken / CISPA Helmholtz Center for Information Security, 2026

Subgroup discovery finds sub-populations deviating from their own population's average, which answers the wrong question when the goal is explaining how two populations differ: a subgroup can look ordinary against the overall distribution yet contain a large gap between the groups inside it. Clinical practice pre-specifies subgroups to preserve power, and data-driven causal alternatives assume randomized trials or structured observational designs. The authors define a differential subgroup by three desiderata: exceptionality, a divergence between the two groups' target distributions inside the subgroup; generality, support in both populations; and covariate independence, so the gap is not attributable to other covariates. DiffSub optimizes their combination by gradient descent over soft conjunctions of per-feature interval conditions, yielding readable rules. On synthetic data it recovers the planted subgroup across observational, randomized, and demographic settings where PySubgroup, SyFlow, UpliftTree and HonestTree each fail somewhere; on IHDP its in-subgroup PEHE is 1.73 against 2.33 and 2.17 for tree baselines, degrading to 3.77 without the covariate penalty. The authors state that unobserved confounders or post-treatment variables break the causal reading, that only one subgroup is returned, and that outputs are hypotheses needing external validation.

### CB-SLICE — Concept-Based Interpretable Error Slice Discovery
`arXiv:2605.29836` · Konforti et al. / University of Cambridge, 2026

Explanation-generating slice discovery methods — Domino, Bias-to-Text, HiBug — outsource their keywords to auxiliary models like CLIP or ClipCap, so the explanation is produced outside the model being debugged, only approximating the true error source and able to inherit the auxiliary model's biases. CB-SLICE instead assumes a Concept Bottleneck Model. It ranks concepts by Expected Change in Target Prediction — how far the label distribution shifts when a concept is intervened to 0 or 1, averaged class-wise over misclassified validation samples — then fits a Gaussian mixture to the concept-logit vectors of erroneous samples restricted to those concepts, adding two auxiliary classifier losses so slice posteriors predict both true and predicted concept values. Slices are labeled by Expected Change in Slice Assignment and ranked by misprediction coherence and semantic compactness. On Waterbirds with a sequential CBM it reaches 0.78 Precision@10 and 0.70 Matched Ground-Truth Group Frequency, against Domino's 0.72/0.17 and GEORGE's 0.22/0.13 — up to roughly 65 points over GEORGE; CelebA, MetaShift and MNIST-Sum are also evaluated. The authors state it requires complete, faithful concept annotations, degrades on noisy ones, and adds CBM training cost; it cannot run on a vanilla DNN.

### NCT — Discovering Latent Groups for Robust Classification
`arXiv:2606.23609` · Garg, Aïvodji, Ebrahimi Kahou & Michalski, 2026

Supervised (GroupDRO), validation-supervised (JTT, EIIL, DFR, CnC) and unsupervised (GEORGE, ExMap, LfF, XRM) robustness methods all adjust network parameters, leaving the classifier opaque at inference: it emits a class and nothing about which latent subgroup a sample belongs to, and never structurally separates conflicting features. Neural Classification Trees put the discovered partition into the architecture. A shared backbone feeds a growing binary tree of heads; at each iteration a sample gets a binary difficulty indicator (1 if misclassified at its current node) and is routed ℓ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾=2ℓ⁽ᵗ⁾+d, correct samples to an easy child, errors to a higher-capacity hard child, with the path string encoding its group. Training alternates a frozen-backbone phase and full fine-tuning, with an auxiliary BCE term preserving parent validity; depth stops via sparse-node merging and a pseudo-WGA criterion with a Wilson bound. On Waterbirds, CelebA, ISIC, UMNIST and CMNIST, worst-group accuracy is competitive and ISIC AUROC exceeds GroupDRO without training group labels; hard leaves capture 82.2% of landbird-on-water, 74.4% of waterbird-on-land, 73.3% of blond-male and 83.7% of color-conflicting CMNIST. The authors flag reliance on simplicity bias — if minority features are easy, routing fails — and on pWGA₂ tracking true WGA.

### Shortcut Groups — Discovery and Spatial Characterisation of Multiple Shortcut Groups for Auditing Vision Model Bias
`arXiv:2608.14051` · Achara et al. / King's College London, 2026

Out-of-distribution testing shows that a vision model fails on a subpopulation without saying which image regions carry the shortcut, and conditional-alignment attribution methods that do localize shortcuts average their evidence into one dataset-level map, erasing patterns appearing in only some images. Here "spatial" means within-image pixel regions, not geography. The method ranks LRP or AttnLRP attributions over per-image grid cells, computes shortcut alignment as the partial correlation between the task model and a sensitive-attribute model controlling for a bias-reduced model (task alignment is the converse), yields per-image shortcut and task contribution maps, and groups the concatenated maps into eight shortcut groups by K-means or NMF. Across CelebA, CheXpert, Waterbirds, Camelyon17 and ISIC2019 on ResNet50 and ViT-B/16, prototypes localize sensibly (hair versus background, lung versus breast shadow), high-error groups reach 90% top-10 error, and the top 20% of images by group risk score holds far more errors than the 20% expected at random. Iterative group-level suppression-plus-amplification cut the largest subgroup gap by 9.8 to 20.3 points. The authors note only one sensitive attribute is studied, grid partitioning assumes rough image alignment, and positive contribution is not causal evidence.

## 4. Datasets and benchmarks

Candidate testbeds, plus the two suites OODSelect itself ran on. The recurring
finding across all of them is that ERM is hard to beat and that specialized
robustness methods tend to close the ID–OOD gap by lowering ID accuracy.

### Folktables — Retiring Adult: New Datasets for Fair Machine Learning
`arXiv:2108.04884` · Ding, Hardt, Miller & Schmidt / UC Berkeley & Toyota Research Institute, NeurIPS 2021

Fairness work on tabular data had converged on UCI Adult, a 1994 CPS extract whose $50,000 threshold sits at the 76th income quantile overall but the 88th among Black and 89th among female respondents, so models score *higher* on those groups than overall. The authors reconstruct it from IPUMS CPS and derive five ACS PUMS tasks: ACSIncome (income above $50,000; columns AGEP, COW, SCHL, MAR, OCCP, POBP, RELP, WKHP, SEX, RAC1P; 1,664,500 rows in 2018), ACSPublicCoverage, ACSMobility, ACSEmployment (3,236,107 rows) and ACSTravelTime. PUMS records carry state (ST) and Public Use Microdata Area (PUMA, areas of 100,000-plus people, codes 100–70301); each task covers 50 states plus Puerto Rico over survey years 2014–2018, giving 255 datasets per task. GBM reaches 79.7% on ACSIncome against a 63.1% constant baseline, but effect sizes swing sharply by state: LFR cuts demographic-parity violation roughly 10 points in some states and near zero in others, post-processing costs under 1% accuracy in some and nearer 5% in others, and cross-state transfer moves accuracy and fairness unpredictably in both directions. The authors note their datasets are entirely US-centric and built for benchmarking rather than substantive measurement of inequality.

### TableShift — Benchmarking Distribution Shift in Tabular Data with TableShift
`arXiv:2312.07577` · Gardner et al. / University of Washington & AI2, NeurIPS 2023 D&B

Robustness benchmarks existed for images and text, but tabular data — where gradient-boosted trees remain state of the art and features are structured rather than raw — had only IID benchmarks, so tabular behaviour under shift was unmeasured. TableShift assembles 15 real, open, binary classification tasks, each split on a named domain variable: school (ASSISTments), institution type (College Scorecard), insurance type (ICU mortality and length of stay), race (diabetes), geographic region (voting, food stamps, income at $56k), education level (unemployment), third-party risk (FICO HELOC), disability status (public coverage), poverty level (childhood lead), BMI category (hypertension). Ten tasks support domain generalization. Nineteen methods were tuned over 100 HyperOpt trials on in-domain validation accuracy, then scored on ID and OOD test splits. Tuned tree baselines lose between 1.25 and 34.49 accuracy points under shift; ID and OOD accuracy correlate at ρ=0.81, and ID accuracy plus label shift Δy predict OOD accuracy at R²=0.996. No model consistently beat XGBoost, LightGBM or CatBoost, and robustness methods shrank gaps by lowering ID accuracy rather than raising OOD. The authors caution that conclusions extend only to shifts resembling TableShift's, that hybrid robust-loss-plus-architecture methods went untested, and that no theoretical link is established.

### WILDS — A Benchmark of in-the-Wild Distribution Shifts
`arXiv:2012.07421` · Koh, Sagawa et al. / Stanford & UC Berkeley, ICML 2021

Existing shift benchmarks induced shifts synthetically (recoloring MNIST, corrupting ImageNet) or by disparate splits (cartoons to photos), and robustness to one kind of shift demonstrably fails to transfer to another. WILDS curates 10 datasets whose splits follow a naturally occurring domain variable — hospital (Camelyon17), camera trap (iWildCam), country and rural/urban (PovertyMap), demographic (CivilComments), molecular scaffold (OGB-MolPCBA), time and region (FMoW), git repository (Py150), user (Amazon), location and time (GlobalWheat), experimental batch (RxRx1) — spanning 6,515 to 539,502 examples and 5 to 120,084 domains, with targets from tumor detection to asset wealth to code autocompletion. ERM drops on every dataset: Camelyon17 93.2→70.3 average accuracy, CivilComments 92.2→56.0 worst-group, Amazon 71.9→53.8 tenth-percentile, iWildCam 47.0→31.0 macro F1, FMoW 48.6→32.3 worst-region. CORAL, IRM and Group DRO fail to beat ERM anywhere except CivilComments, where Group DRO reaches 70.0 but stays far below the 92.2 ID number; IRM actively collapses on iWildCam (15.1) and RxRx1 (8.2). The authors warn that several datasets have few test domains — Camelyon17 has one OOD hospital — inviting overfitting to specific test sets.

### DomainBed — In Search of Lost Domain Generalization
`arXiv:2007.01434` · Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz / Facebook AI Research, ICLR 2021

Domain generalization papers used different datasets, architectures and — critically — undisclosed model-selection protocols, so reported gains over baselines could not be attributed to the algorithms. DomainBed fixes all three. It supplies seven multi-domain image classification datasets whose domain variable is explicit: color-label correlation (Colored MNIST), rotation angle (Rotated MNIST), source corpus (VLCS: Caltech101/LabelMe/SUN09/VOC2007), rendering style (PACS, Office-Home, DomainNet's six domains over 345 classes) and camera-trap location (Terra Incognita). Nine algorithms (ERM, IRM, Group DRO, Mixup, MLDG, CORAL, MMD, DANN, C-DANN) run under three selection rules — training-domain validation, leave-one-domain-out CV, and a test-domain oracle capped at 20 queries — with ResNet-50 backbones, frozen batch-norm, strong augmentation and a 20-trial random search, the whole study repeated three times for 45,900 networks. Under training-domain validation ERM averages 67.0 and nothing exceeds it by more than a point (CORAL 67.2, Mixup 67.1, IRM 66.0, Group DRO 65.5); oracle selection lifts every method roughly two points. The authors question whether these datasets reflect realistic deployment at all, and note every DG method rests on an untestable invariance assumption.

### GEO-Bench-2 — From Performance to Capability, Rethinking Evaluation in Geospatial AI
`arXiv:2511.15658` · Simumba, Lehmann, Fraccaro et al. / IBM Research Europe, TU Munich & ServiceNow, 2025

Geospatial foundation model evaluation had no shared protocol: PANGAEA and Copernicus-Bench cover narrow modalities or carry restrictive licenses, and the original GEO-Bench omitted detection and instance segmentation, lacked fine-tuning tooling, and collapsed everything into one aggregate score that hid where models actually fail. GEO-Bench-2 gathers 19 permissively licensed datasets across classification, semantic segmentation, pixel regression, object detection and instance segmentation — BEN V2 (20000/4000/4000, 19 classes), TreeSatAI, So2Sat, ForestNet, BioMassters, CaFFe, CloudSEN12, NASA Burn Scars (524/160/120), Dynamic EarthNet, FLAIR 2, FTW, KuroSiwo, PASTIS, SpaceNet2/7, EverWatch, NZCattle, Substations — subsampled to at most 20,000 classification or 5,000 pixel-wise samples, grouped into nine overlapping capabilities rather than one score, and scored by 16-trial Optuna tuning plus five refits under normalized bootstrapped IQM. No model dominates: Clay-V1 ViT-B (86M) tops Core and Pixel-wise, ImageNet ConvNeXt and DINOv3 lead high-resolution RGB, while TerraMind-V1-Large leads Multi-Temporal and Multi-Spectral, where dropping non-RGB bands costs up to 10% on Burn Scars and PASTIS. The authors flag a missing SAR capability despite five SAR datasets, Europe/North America bias, and no assessment of predictive uncertainty.

### GEO-Bench — Toward Foundation Models for Earth Monitoring
`arXiv:2306.03831` · Lacoste, Lehmann et al. / ServiceNow Research, TU Munich & Stanford, NeurIPS 2023 D&B

Earth observation data differs from natural images — overhead view, satellite revisits, thirteen Sentinel-2 bands, SAR, georeferencing — so CLIP-style pretrained models transfer poorly, and every pretraining paper evaluated on its own narrow task set with weak uncertainty reporting, making progress unmeasurable. GEO-Bench packages 12 tasks: six classification (m-bigearthnet, 43 classes, 20000/1000/1000, 12 bands; m-so2sat, 17 classes, 19992/986/986, 18 bands; m-brick-kiln, 15063 train; m-forestnet, 6464; m-eurosat, deliberately cut to 2000 train; m-pv4ger, 11814 at 0.1 m) and six segmentation (m-pv4ger-seg, m-chesapeake-landcover, m-cashew-plantation, m-SA-crop-type, m-nz-cattle at 524 train, m-NeonTree at 270), spanning 0.1–30 m/pixel with classes rebalanced and splits kept spatially non-overlapping. Evaluation is 16 tuning trials then 10 seeds, aggregated by bootstrapped interquartile mean. ConvNeXt-B and SwinV2-T beat the other classification baselines almost everywhere; remote-sensing pretraining (MoCo-S2, SeCo-S2) does not beat ImageNet weights on RGB; ConvNeXt-B-timm matches a random-init ResNet18 using 2% of the data. The authors state it does not test temporal fine-tuning or multi-modal fusion, that spatial coverage needs many more countries and biomes, and that error bars will overlap as models approach the aleatoric limit.

### SustainBench — Benchmarks for Monitoring the Sustainable Development Goals with Machine Learning
`arXiv:2111.04724` · Yeh, Meng, Wang et al. / Caltech, Stanford & UC Berkeley, NeurIPS 2021 D&B

Only about half of SDG indicators have regular data from more than half the world's countries, and prior satellite-based SDG work used incompatible datasets and metrics while requiring remote-sensing expertise most ML researchers lack. SustainBench compiles 15 tasks across 7 SDGs, 11 released publicly for the first time: asset wealth over space (86,936 examples, 48 countries) and over time (1,665), cropland mapping (200,000), crop type (2,968 and 39,762), yield (11,048), field delineation (1,966), child mortality (105,582), women's BMI (94,866), women's education (117,062), water (87,938) and sanitation (89,271) indices, brick kiln detection (86,528), land-cover representation learning, and out-of-domain land cover over 692 regions. Inputs span Landsat, Sentinel-1/2, MODIS, PlanetScope, Mapillary street imagery and DHS/LSMS surveys; DHS tasks split by country, with 30 training countries and disjoint validation and test country sets. Baselines spread widely: r²=0.63 for asset wealth but 0.01 for child mortality, 0.42 BMI, 0.26 education, 0.40 water; crop-type macro-F1 0.70 in South Sudan versus 0.30 in Kenya; out-of-domain land cover kappa 0.32. The authors note datasets stay far smaller than canonical ML corpora, many SDGs are absent, and imperfect predictions may propagate bias into policy.

### TFM-OOD — Empirical Evaluation of Out-Of-Distribution Performance of Tabular Foundation Models
`arXiv:2607.26000` · Loza, Chushig-Muzo et al. / USFQ, Rey Juan Carlos & Universidad Latina de Panamá, 2026

Tabular foundation models are trained and benchmarked almost entirely under IID assumptions; TableShift characterised shift behaviour for trees and tabular neural networks but never touched TFMs, leaving open whether pretraining on real rather than synthetic corpora buys distributional robustness. The study evaluates nine TFMs — TabPFN v2/v2.5/v2.6/v3 and TabFM (real-data pretraining), Mitra (mixed SCM plus tree-ensemble prior), TabICL, TabICLv2 and LimiX (synthetic SCM priors) — on three TableShift datasets: HELOC (label shift, 38 features, 2,220 train / 278 ID test / 6,914 OOD test), Childhood Lead (poverty-level socioeconomic shift, 17 features, 11,807 / 1,476 / 11,466) and Voting (US Census region, South held out, 380 features, 37,548 / 4,694 / 23,103). Models run zero-shot at default settings with the training split as in-context examples, scored by ROC-AUC over five seeds. Every model degrades, with shift gaps of 0.003–0.060; real-data pretraining gives the best absolute OOD AUC (mean ≈0.79) but the *largest* mean gap (≈0.019) versus mixed ≈0.015 and synthetic ≈0.012, so the advantage is ID fitting. Structurally, only three of TableShift's fifteen tasks are used, no tuning or tree baseline is included, and TabFM and LimiX are absent from Voting for memory reasons.

## 5. GeoAI explainability and spatial statistics

The methodological machinery for stage 2, and the reasons to distrust it. These
six are the least glamorous papers in the library and the most likely to decide
whether a "this subset is explained by X" claim survives review.

### Prediction-Domain Adaptive Evaluation — Moving beyond spatial and random cross-validation in environmental modelling
`arXiv:2605.13689` · Linnenbrink & Nowosad et al. / University of Münster, 2026

Random cross-validation on clustered training data places each test point inside the autocorrelation range of its neighbours, so fold nearest-neighbour distances mirror training-to-training distances rather than the far larger prediction-to-training distances the map actually faces; accuracy is overestimated and overfitting stays invisible, because the structures the model overfitted to are still present in the test data. Spatial block CV overcorrects, manufacturing extrapolation the model never meets. The authors argue real cases lie on a continuum between these poles, quantified as the Wasserstein distance between those two nearest-neighbour distance densities, and reproduce Wadoux et al.'s Amazon above-ground-biomass simulation — random forest, 28 climatic and remote-sensing predictors, 100 repetitions — extending it from two designs to four: random (W=1), weakly clustered (W=31), strongly clustered (W=74), extrapolation (W=462). kNNDM, their exemplar of prediction-domain adaptive CV, tracks true RMSE across every design except full extrapolation, where it and spatial CV both turn optimistic; restricting predictions to the area of applicability pulls estimates back toward truth. They stress that no cross-validation substitutes for a probability sample, and that explanation-oriented modelling likely needs different strategies entirely.

### Spatial Confounding Review — A review of concepts, challenges, and current approaches
`arXiv:2602.17792` · Pim et al. / Getulio Vargas Foundation & UFMG, 2026

Spatial confounding is the non-identifiability that appears when a smoothly varying covariate and a flexible spatial random effect compete for the same signal. Under the generating model Y(s) = β₀ + β_X X(s) + Z(s) + ε(s) with Z unobserved and spatially structured, omitting Z biases β_X, while absorbing it into a CAR or Gaussian-process term attenuates coefficients, inflates standard errors, and can flip significance. The review organises the field into spatial filtering (Getis-Ord, Moran eigenvectors); restricted spatial regression, which projects the random effect onto the orthogonal complement of col(X) (criticised for interval undercoverage); Paciorek's scale-of-confounding result that GLS carries exactly the OLS bias ρ(σ_z/σ_x)β_z, so spatial modelling helps only when the confounded range is the broader one; scale-based adjustments such as Spatial+, gSEM and spectral adjustment; and causal approaches including double machine learning. A standardised head-to-head then runs on six real datasets, among them Scotland lip cancer, Pennsylvania lung cancer and Gambia malaria. RSR generally stays near the non-spatial estimate; Spatial+ shrinks Slovenian socio-economic status to nil because its signal sits in the low frequencies Spatial+ removes; SPOCK behaves anomalously on Pennsylvania. Stated gaps: extreme sensitivity to misspecified linearity and homogeneity, bootstrap uncertainty ill-suited to dependent data, and no way to assess basis-choice sensitivity.

### GeoShapley Encoder Benchmark — Do Location Encoders Capture Spatial Effects? A GeoShapley Benchmark Across Scales
`arXiv:2606.23453` · Kiv & Wang / UIUC, SIGSPATIAL 2026

A location encoder spreads geographic information across all D embedding dimensions, none corresponding to an interpretable geographic quantity, so feature-wise explainers divide attribution D ways and never recover a coherent location effect. GeoShapley solves this by grouping every location feature into one joint GEO player, but had only been tested on raw coordinates and analytic bases. Eleven TorchSpatial encoders at D=8 — Space2Vec, tile_ffn, wrap_ffn, NeRF, five Sphere2Vec variants, random Fourier features — are benchmarked against a synthetic process y = β₀(s) + β₁(s)X₁ + β₂(s)X₂ + ε on a 25×25 grid (N=625), county centroids (N≈3,000), and a global sample (N=10,000), scoring recovery as Pearson r between estimated and true coefficients over 25 seeds. β₁ recovers at r > 0.98 nearly everywhere; β₂ separates encoders only globally (Sphere2Vec-sphereM+ 0.991, wrap_ffn 0.988, Sphere2Vec-dfs 0.982 against a coordinate-only baseline of 0.926), with grid and county gains ≤ 0.02. Contrastive training usually degrades recovery slightly. Dropping coordinates inverts the ranking — the global leaders fall to r ≈ 0.56–0.64 on the grid. The authors note the process is synthetic and smooth, D is fixed at 8, and results are specific to the MLP and explainer settings.

### Target-Weighted Cross-Validation — Aligning Validation with Deployment in Spatial Prediction
`arXiv:2603.29981` · Brenning & Suesse / FSU Jena & ELLIS Unit Jena, 2026

Cross-validation implicitly assumes validation observations represent the prediction tasks met at deployment. Preferential and clustered monitoring networks break this twice: the predictor distribution at sampled sites differs from the domain (covariate shift), and so does the distribution of prediction distances (task-difficulty shift), so CV scores the tasks the network defines rather than those the map requires. The authors split CV into a task generator and a risk estimator, then fix the second. Each task is described by T = (x, d), with d the distance to the nearest training point; TWCV solves calibration (raking) equations Σwᵢg(Tᵢ) = m so the weighted margins of discretised descriptors match the target grid's — a low-dimensional stand-in for density-ratio importance weighting. Buffered leave-m-out with varying radii supplies coverage. Over 100 replicates on a heteroskedastic unit-square simulation (n=200, random forest and regression-kriging, three sampling designs), only buffered TWCV and buffered IWCV stay nearly unbiased; LOOCV runs optimistic, LOBOCV pessimistic, and DWCV and spatial kNNDM correct clustering only. On 503 German NO₂ stations, half within the 2% densest areas, TWCV lands 15–35% below other estimators. The authors stress positivity: without validation tasks covering the deployment support, weighting destabilises — the case study's effective sample size was 63.

### SHAP_Geo — Reframing Spatial Dependence as Geographic Feature Attribution
`arXiv:2506.16996` · Chen & Luo / Technical University of Munich & MIT, 2025

Spatial dependence is either measured descriptively by Moran's I and LISA, which are not built for prediction, or assumed and encoded as a distance-decay weight matrix; neither gives a data-driven account of what location itself contributes. The authors reread it as the predictive power of coordinates alone, defining SHAP_Geo_i = φ_x,i + φ_y,i, the summed SHAP values for latitude and longitude from a model trained only on coordinates, which by SHAP's efficiency property equals ẑ_i − μ_ẑ, then correlating |SHAP_Geo| with local Moran's I. Synthetic tests use a 100×100 grid (n=10,000) spanning latitude [10,50] and longitude [−120,−70] across five patterns, LISA with 8-nearest-neighbour weights, and XGBoost; the real test is California housing, 20,640 block groups, seven variables. Synthetic Pearson correlations exceed 0.94 throughout, peaking for horizontal (0.9679), V-shaped (0.9548) and radial (0.9476) patterns. California gives 0.64–0.88, with bivariate Moran's I from 0.34 to 0.86, strongest for MedHouseValue (0.8781, 0.8604) and weakest for Population. Structurally, since SHAP_Geo is by construction the centred coordinate-only prediction, the correlation mostly measures how well XGBoost interpolates a smooth surface; the synthetic patterns are noiseless global trends, and one learner and one real dataset carry the validation.

### GeoAggregator Efficiency — Improving the Computational Efficiency and Explainability of GeoAggregator
`arXiv:2507.17977` · Deng et al. / University of Glasgow & Florida State University, SIGSPATIAL 2025

GeoAggregator scales linearly in theory, but the original implementation rebuilds k-d tree neighbour queries on the fly at every training epoch and inference pass, and its Gaussian-kernel attention carries one learnable bias factor λ shared across all heads, capping the range of scales multi-head attention can represent. Three changes follow: a per-head λ^(h) in α^(h)_ij = softmax((W_Q^(h)e_i)ᵀW_K^(h)e_j − λ^(h)d²_ij); a data-loading factory with separate context and query pools that precomputes and caches neighbours, and at inference widens the search radius then randomly drops surplus context points so repeated passes can be ensembled; and a get_shap_predictor wrapper giving GeoShapley a predictive interface, keyed by per-point IDs so true neighbours survive perturbation of the location features. Across the eight synthetic datasets from the original GA paper, ensembling without retraining lifts mean R² from 0.841 to 0.844 and cuts MAE from 1.149 to 1.135; caching gives an average 36% inference speedup on SL-r. On GWR-r, GA's recovered coefficient surfaces are smoother than XGBoost's, which show noise and hard decision boundaries. The authors note that explaining GA costs roughly 1,800 s against XGBoost's 100 s and call for a GPU-friendly GeoShapley.

## 6. Synthesis — what these 40 papers add up to

**The line is real, tight, and should be expected on ACS.** Miller reports R² of
0.997 on CIFAR-10.2 and 0.996 on ImageNet-V2; more to the point, TableShift finds
that on *tabular* data with *geographic* domain splits, ID accuracy plus label
shift predicts OOD accuracy at R² = 0.996. That is nearly the strongest
accuracy-on-the-line result in the library, and it is measured on almost exactly
the setting this project proposes to work in. Good news, in that there has to be
a line for OODSelect to break. Sobering news, in that the aggregate story on
tabular geographic shift is very strong.

**Three different mechanisms break the line, and they are not the same claim.**
Teney breaks it by sampling models densely along training trajectories rather
than across seeds. Sanyal breaks it by interpolating label noise in a large
nuisance-feature space, with a lower bound to explain why. Salaudeen breaks it by
disaggregating. Any paper claiming inversion has to say which mechanism it is
invoking, and Sanyal's is a live confound here: ACS income is self-reported
survey data thresholded at $50,000, so label noise is not hypothetical. An
OODSelect result on ACS that does not check whether it survives early stopping
and stronger regularization — Sanyal's non-interpolating controls, which stayed
positive — is not yet a result about spurious correlations.

**The field's bottleneck is naming, not finding.** Read the limitations sections
in §3 together: Domino's web-trained embeddings encode biases that govern which
groups get flagged; LADDER inherits CLIP and LLM bias; CB-SLICE needs complete,
faithful concept annotations; SWD needs a pre-specified ODD; OODSelect's subsets

Geospatial data hands you that table for free, from public sources, with no
labeling budget and no auxiliary model to inherit bias from. That is the
structural argument for this project, and it should be made in those terms.

**But the geo literature says that table is treacherous.** Spatial confounding is
non-identifiability: a smoothly varying covariate and a flexible spatial random
effect compete for the same signal, and Paciorek's result means spatial modelling
only helps when the confounded range is the broader one. The review's own case
study is a warning — Spatial+ shrank a real Slovenian socio-economic effect to
nil because that signal lived in the low frequencies Spatial+ removes. "This
subset is explained by urbanicity" is exactly the claim that literature says is
hardest to make honestly.

**Random CV will lie, and the magnitude is known.** TWCV lands 15–35% below every
other estimator on 503 real German NO₂ stations. The Amazon biomass simulation
puts the Wasserstein gap between fold distances and prediction distances at 1 for
random designs and 462 for extrapolation. Budget for kNNDM or TWCV from the
start; retrofitting it after the stage-2 numbers exist means redoing them.

**The whole-state baseline is more threatening than it looks.** The mobility
paper found mean CPC across source states spans about 0.07 — explicitly noting
that range exceeds published gaps between competing architectures — and that
transfer is asymmetric, with CPC asymmetry up to 0.2. Folktables documents the
same pattern from the fairness side: interventions that cut demographic-parity
violation ~10 points in some states do nothing in others, and cross-state
transfer moves both accuracy and fairness unpredictably in both directions.
Geography already explains a great deal before any subset is selected. The
interesting finding would be *sub-state* structure, and the whole-state baseline
is the only thing that separates the two.

**The most actionable methodological lesson is Teney's, and it is about
sampling.** In vision, model-population diversity comes from architecture and
pretraining. On tabular there is neither — and EarthShift and GEO-Bench both find
domain-specific pretraining buys nothing over ImageNet weights anyway, while the
GFM audit found the same model-benchmark pair reported 56.6 points apart. So
diversity has to come from algorithm family and, critically, from *where along
training* models are sampled. Teney's finding is that plotting fixed-epoch models
across seeds reproduces exactly the vertical scatter that hides inversion, while
varying epochs within a seed reveals it. Sample along trajectories, not just
across seeds — otherwise a null result may be an artifact of the model
population rather than a fact about the data.
